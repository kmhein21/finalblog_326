* how each one ties into class content

All of the mini-projects that we had in this course tie directly into the specific sections that they were designed to follow. After each completion of a section, the mini-project helped solidify theoretical ideas which related to the main themes of the course. There are aspects though where we see the mini-projects connecting through the sections too, and looking back at all the coursework we can see how certain projects had us prepare for certain sections or return to ideas from previous sections.

Mini-project 1 connected heavily to the first section of notes on sampling distributions. In this project we simulated distributions for the minimum and maximum of various probability distributions. This connected both the ideas we saw in the first note packet, sampling distributions and order statistics. In the labs for the first section, we used statistical simulation to look at how sampling distributions could change. This mini-project was directly similar to this concept, just a bit more complex as we used more than one type of probability distribution. We can also see connections in all of the later sections, as we continuously saw the concept of a sampling distribution reappear, and we also continued to utilize statistical simulation in most of the labs and other mini-projects within the course.

Mini-project 2 seemed to connect to all of the sections we worked through, but I would say it didn’t really appear too much in our Bayesian statistics section. This mini-project solidified our understanding of extremely important vocabulary, thus creating a foundation we could expand upon in later sections. A majority of these words had been introduced to us in our section on estimation (bias, consistent, variance, etc.) but there was also some which we had learned in section 1. As we worked through the class we continued to see a lot of these words (such as likelihood, parameter). Naturally, this mini-project connected to section 2 on estimation, where we derived a lot of estimators and estimates for different distributions and focused on their variance and bias. This project differed from the others because it didn’t have any similar computations to the section that it followed, it was strictly completed to increase our knowledge of the vocabulary.

Mini-project 3 connected to the section based on confidence intervals, but it also had some overlap with section 5 on hypothesis testing. In this mini-project, we saw ways that the intervals would vary when given different parameters (proportion and sample size). This expanded on the notes and examples we worked through in section 3, where we learned how to calculate and interpret confidence intervals based on data, but the mini-project helped incorporate statistical simulation and understand how confidence intervals would react to unmet assumptions. This idea of breaking or meeting assumptions tied into the section on hypothesis testing, as we saw assumptions as being very important for test statistics which rely on the Central Limit Theorem.

Mini-project 4 was unique, as it really only connected to the section 4 coursework. This section was different because it focused specifically on Bayesian statistics, and not frequentist statistics like all of the other sections. During this section, we focused on deriving posterior distributions from various prior distributions. These prior distributions were either non-informative or were created using a priori knowledge. Mini-project 4 directly relates to this idea as we used various informative and non-informative priors to display how they can alter the posterior distribution. Another way that this connected was through the creation of informative priors, which we could make based on knowledge from ‘experts’ and this was done in both our section coursework and the mini-project.

mini-project 5

Our last mini-project was quite unique, like the mini-project from section 2 it didn’t have to do with computations. This mini-project was basically an analysis of the paper ‘Moving to a world beyond p<0.05’ by Wasserstein, Schirm, and Lazar. In this project we focused on understanding why using p-values and confidence intervals may not be the best practice, and how these values are seen as extremely reliable when in reality they are made based on data, not the truth. In the notes for section 5, we spend a lot of time learning how to calculate and interpret p-values, so this paper added a layer to our understanding of how we should be drawing conclusions and looking at results.

* biggest takeaways from the projects

Each of these mini-projects had valuable lessons which applied to our coursework, but there were specific things that I took away from them.

mini-project 1:

The biggest takeaway that I took from the second mini-project is a little less theoretical. By writing this meaningful story I was able to work all of the vocab words together, and this helped strengthen my understanding of what they really mean (aside from a strict definition). The biggest takeaway that I took from the third mini-project relates to how broken assumptions will affect confidence intervals. When these assumptions are broken it can alter the width and coverage rate of the interval, which then affects the accuracy of your confidence interval interpretation. The biggest takeaway that I took from the fourth mini-project relates to how Bayesian statistics differs from frequentist statistics. Through this, mini-project I learned how influential the prior distribution is to the posterior distribution, and that by using different prior distributions you can create entirely different posterior distributions (which will then affect your conclusions).
